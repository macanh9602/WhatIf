## Premise
At night a tornado tears the walls off your grandma's mobile home and carries you, knocked out, over a fence and into a field. You wake in the grass with a cut head and scratched feet. A weather service man measures the ride with a GPS: 1,307 feet, the farthest anyone has been carried by a tornado and lived. The twist: the ride was not what hurt you. Tornadoes kill with what they throw, and your one real hit came from the heavy lamp in the living room, before you ever left it.

## Beats
- Paradox (hook, 14 words): "Tonight, a storm tears the walls off your trailer, and then it takes you." The consequence is in frame from frame 0: the walls peeling away around you, your feet leaving the floor.
- Plant: grandma's lamp, "big, heavy and ugly", on the side table next to the couch. Reads as a joke about the decor.
- Count up: the front door, then the back door, then the windows (from his own account, ABC 2006), then your head, then the walls, then you.
- Mockery (light, invented, aimed at the claim, not the man): the neighbor at the door, "You came from where?", and "Nobody believes how far you went." Sits at ~60 % (later than the skill's 15-35 %): the only honest place for doubt in this story is after the landing.
- Turn: "You wake up in soft grass, in the rain, alone. No trailer in sight." Then the measurement: thirteen hundred and seven feet, a record.
- You see (word ~150 of 222, ~68 %): the ride was not the danger; a tornado kills with what it throws; the doctor says you were lifted, not dragged; just a cut head and scratched feet; the worst hit came from inside the room. "It was the lamp." The plant flips from a decor joke to the one thing that hurt you.
- Button: "The storm took the walls, the windows and you. The lamp just got there first." Reuses "storm", "walls" and "took/takes" from the hook. Loop line: none (the seam "...got there first. | Tonight, a storm tears the walls..." replays cleanly but is not one sentence).

## Changed from the user's brief, and why
- Brief hook "A tornado carried a man 1,307 feet. He lived." Two fixes. (1) "He lived" answers the hook at 2 s (rule: no answer before 20 s); the script lets you wake up at ~30 s and says "lived" only at the record line. (2) Rules want "you" in the hook and the consequence on screen; "a man" is a third person. The number moves to its own slow line after the GPS walk.
- "Trailer park at dusk": wrong on both counts. It was his grandmother's mobile home near Fordland, in the country (Guinness, ClickOrlando), not a park; and the F2 north of Fordland ran about 11:03-11:20 PM (NWS Springfield event page). So: one mobile home among fields, NIGHT. The survey moves to the next morning (dawn light is our choice; the date of the GPS walk is not in any source I opened, the script does not say when).
- "Survived with cuts and bruises": the sources say a head injury from the lamp (bleeding, knocked out) and badly scratched feet, otherwise unhurt. Script: "Just a cut head and scratched feet."
- "Woke up in a field": right, "a field of soft grass" (Guinness), "woke up in a grassy field ... after being thrown over a barbed wire fence" (ClickOrlando).
- Why he survived: the brief's "carried, not dropped from height" is not in any source I opened (no height is published). What IS sourced: his doctor said the injuries suggested he was lifted into the air rather than blown across the ground (Guinness), and NOAA SPC: "Flying debris is the greatest danger in tornadoes." The script uses only those two.

## True / invented
"You" is a stand-in for Matt Suter, 19, a real man; the shots of what he saw in the air are imagined (he was unconscious). Invented: the "You came from where?" line, "nobody believes", the lamp's look ("ugly" is ours; "heavy" is sourced), the rain shell surveyor, the dawn, the doctor's scene (his finding is real). Description draft: "'You' stands in for a real person: on 12 March 2006, 19-year-old Matt Suter was knocked out by a lamp in his grandmother's mobile home near Fordland, Missouri, when an F2 tornado hit. He woke in a field 1,307 feet (398 m) away, measured by the National Weather Service with a GPS, the farthest distance anyone is known to have survived being carried by a tornado (Guinness World Records). His injuries: a head wound and scratched feet. Flying debris is the greatest danger in a tornado (NOAA Storm Prediction Center). If you live in a mobile home, get out to a sturdy shelter before a tornado comes."
