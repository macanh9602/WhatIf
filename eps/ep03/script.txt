If every level up cost you a year of your life, teenagers would become the richest people on Earth.
Imagine waking up to a little glowing menu: plus one level, minus one year.
One level makes you about ten percent better at anything, and the year comes off the very end, when you're old.
So of course you take it. Then another.
By lunch you can run a marathon, speak three languages, and your life just got ten years shorter.
Soon the whole world is doing the math, and the math is strange.
A twenty-year-old has sixty years to spend, so teenagers become superheroes overnight.
Your grandma has maybe five years left, so she sticks to level one and guards them like gold.
The Olympics turn into a contest of who will spend the most years, so every gold medal comes with a very short retirement.
And banks start offering loans in years, with interest.
You see, the trick is that a year at eighty feels worthless when you're twenty.
Brains discount the future, so a reward today beats the same reward years from now, which is exactly why credit cards work.
And here's the strange part: nature already plays this game.
Big dogs level up their size, and they pay for it with years.
A Great Dane grows to seventy kilos and usually lives about eight years.
A Chihuahua stays tiny and often makes it to fifteen.
One study found every two extra kilos cost a dog about a month of life.
So be honest: how many levels would you buy?
